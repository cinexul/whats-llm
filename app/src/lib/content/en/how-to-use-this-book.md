Welcome to this book. Before we start, take three minutes to read this page. It tells you where this book wants to take you, how you can read it, and one mindset that will save you a lot of detours.

This book is for people who know their way around a computer but have no background in AI or programming. If you can send email and use a browser and office software, you can read it just fine. **You don't need to know how to code.** When we reach the chapters where you build a project hands-on, we'll show you how to direct the AI in plain language, not make you memorize commands.

## 1. Where this book wants to take you

By the time you finish, you'll have four things, going from simple to deep:

| | What you'll learn | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Understand it | What a large language model is, why it can hold a conversation, why it gets things wrong. No more being intimidated by jargon | Part One |
| 2. Use it | Putting AI to work in daily life, study, and your job: asking well, giving it the right material, not getting led astray | Part Two |
| 3. See clearly | Model, product, API, Agent, RAG, local models: one map of the whole tool ecosystem | Part Three |
| 4. Build | Using Claude Code and Codex, the two mainstream tools, to build your own projects | Parts Four and Five |

Part Six, at the end, is about risk and limits: what you can hand to AI and what you never should, and how to use it responsibly without wasting money or stepping into traps.

This book isn't a list of tips and tricks. We want you to understand how AI works, so you can decide for yourself when to trust it and when to check its work. Once you understand the principles, the techniques come easily.

## 2. Two ways to read: pick the one that suits you

You don't have to read it cover to cover. Pick the path that fits you:

- You just want to use it: read Parts One and Two (basics and everyday use), then skip to Part Six (risk). That's enough to use AI smoothly and safely.
- You want to build things: on top of that, continue through Parts Three, Four, and Five (tool ecosystem, coding in practice, six real projects). Step by step you'll build a personal website, a document tool, a meeting-notes assistant, and even connect AI to your own program.

Whichever path you take, don't skip Part One. Every later "why use it this way" rests on the principles it explains.

## 3. How to use the extras

- The "Quiz" at the end of each chapter: usually six questions, mostly concept, misconception, and scenario questions, often ending with a hands-on or observation exercise (a few chapters add other types, such as a calculation). For multiple-choice questions, the reader shows the answer and explanation after you pick an option; hands-on and observation exercises have no single right answer, so you can expand a list of points to look for. The explanations of why an answer is wrong, and when people tend to fall for it, are worth more than the score.
- Glossary (Appendix A): a three-language reference (Chinese, Japanese, English). Look up any word you don't know.
- Prompt templates (Appendix B): fill-in-the-blank templates for everyday, work, study, coding, and other situations.
- Numbers at a glance (Appendix E): token counts of the same sentence, context windows of mainstream models, API prices, parameter counts of open models, and more, each with the date it was checked.
- Trilingual (Chinese / Japanese / English): the book comes in Chinese, Japanese, and English editions. Click "中文 / 日本語 / English" at the top of the reader to switch; the content matches one to one.
- The reader also supports jumping through the table of contents on the left, full-text search, and highlighting and notes. Where you've read and what you've marked are saved automatically.

## 4. One mindset that runs through the whole book

If you remember only two sentences, make them these:

1. **AI is very capable, but it can be confidently wrong.** Fluent, confident wording is not proof of correctness. Alignment training, retrieval, and verification tools reduce errors but don't eliminate them. So check important facts, numbers, and quotes yourself (chapter 9 goes into detail). Treat it as a smart, handy assistant that occasionally talks nonsense, not an encyclopedia that's always right.
2. **Current context, saved history, and product "memory" are three different things.** The model itself doesn't remember you from one conversation to the next. When it seems to remember you, it's almost always the product putting saved information back into this conversation (chapters 3 and 9 explain how). Once you see this, the puzzles of "why did it forget?" and "how does it remember?" clear up.

- This is an introduction to large language models and AI tools in practice, written for everyone; no programming background is needed.
- Four goals: understand it, use it, see the tool ecosystem clearly, and build projects with Claude Code and Codex, while keeping risk in check.
- If you just want to use it, read Parts One, Two, and Six; if you want to build, continue with Parts Three, Four, and Five.
