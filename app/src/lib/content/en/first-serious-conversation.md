The first nine chapters covered what the model is and how it works: it adds words one at a time (Chapter 4), doesn't check truth as it goes, and so sometimes makes things up with a straight face (Chapter 9); and the model itself has no memory across conversations (Chapter 3).

But when you actually open the chat box, fingers on the keyboard, the first question you run into is a different one: what should I treat this thing as? An all-knowing expert, a search engine, or an assistant that does work for me? Get that wrong and you either don't dare use it and miss out, or trust it too much and get led astray without noticing.

This chapter adds no new theory; it just helps you get your attitude right. One comparison is enough: treat it as a very well-read intern who now and then makes things up with a straight face.

## 1. Who it's most like: a "well-read intern who also makes things up"

Imagine your company has sent you a new intern with these traits:

| This intern | In model terms |
| --- | --- |
| Has read an amazing amount | The huge body of knowledge learned into its parameters during training (Chapter 2) |
| Fast, tireless, never complains | Ask for ten drafts and you get ten, even at 3 a.m. |
| Very articulate, very confident | It aims to sound right, so its wording is always smooth and sure of itself (Chapter 4) |
| But sometimes makes things up, quite seriously | When it doesn't know, it may not say "I can't," and may instead come up with a plausible fake answer (Chapter 9) |
| Doesn't remember what you told it yesterday | The model itself has no memory across conversations; what it knows in a new one depends on what the product brought in this time (Chapter 3) |
| Doesn't know what you really want | Whatever you don't spell out, it can only guess (the problem Chapter 11 deals with) |

Keep both halves of the comparison in mind: "well-read" and "makes things up." Remember only "well-read" and you'll trust it blindly, as an authority. Remember only "makes things up" and you'll be afraid to hand it anything. Both are true: it knows more than you, but what it says needs your check. The best way to see yourself is as **the person supervising the intern**: you can hand off the work freely, but your name goes on it and the consequences are yours, so you take the final look yourself.

Of course, it isn't actually a person. It has no intentions, doesn't slack off, and doesn't deliberately deceive you. "Intern" is just a comparison to help you get the balance right, so you neither overrate nor underrate it.

## 2. Work you can safely outsource vs work you must backstop yourself

With that balance in mind, the most useful step is to sort which work you can hand off and which you need to keep an eye on. The dividing line picks up from Chapter 9: work where the answer is in the material you gave it, and mistakes are obvious at a glance, you can hand off; work that needs it to come up with hard facts from nothing, where mistakes are costly, you have to backstop.

| | Safe to outsource | You must backstop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical tasks | Rewriting and polishing, rephrasing, first drafts, outlines, brainstorming, summarizing material you pasted in, translating, explaining a concept | Key numbers, citations, names and dates, legal, medical, and financial conclusions, factual content you'll publish |
| Why | Either the answer is in the context you gave it, or mistakes are obvious at a glance and quick to fix | It can make things up that look completely real (Chapter 9), and you're the one who pays for mistakes |
| Your role | Say what you need, then check what comes back | Check it again yourself; it only counts once you've confirmed it |

Two contrasting examples:

```text
Safe to outsource:
   "Rewrite this 300-word notice to sound more conversational and warm, for parents."
   —— You have the material, it's a style task, and mistakes are obvious at a glance.

You must backstop:
   "What exactly is the tax-free threshold under a certain national tax policy?"
   —— A hard fact it might make up on the spot (Chapter 9). Have it give a source and check it yourself,
      or go straight to the government's official website.
```

The easiest place to slip up is work that looks safe to outsource but has hard facts tucked inside. Ask it to "write an email introducing our company's product": writing is its strength, but the moment it slips in a spec, a price, or an award, that part becomes a hard fact. When the draft comes back, first pull out the specific numbers, names, dates, and quotations and check them one by one (Chapter 9 shows how); then use the rest of the text with confidence.

## 3. What to expect, and not expect, the first time

Get your expectations right and you won't be disappointed. Get them wrong and you'll either think it can do everything and get caught out, or think it can do nothing and miss out.

What you can expect:

- It understands natural language. Ask in plain words; there are no commands or magic phrases to learn.
- It produces usable first drafts quickly. Emails, outlines, changes of style, explanations of concepts: often you get something workable in one go.
- It revises tirelessly. Not happy? Ask for another version, another angle, longer or shorter. It never gets tired of being asked.
- It works from material you give it. Paste in documents and have it summarize, compare, or pull things out; this is usually fairly dependable (Chapter 12 goes deeper).

What not to count on:

- Every sentence being true. It makes things up, and the smoother it sounds, the more careful you should be (Chapter 9).
- Remembering yesterday. A new conversation starts from scratch by default; even with a memory feature on, only what was saved gets brought in (Chapter 3).
- Knowing the latest news. Its knowledge has a cutoff (Chapter 9); for recent events, don't trust an answer it gives without looking anything up.
- Reading your mind. Requirements you don't spell out, it can only guess (Chapter 11 shows you how to state them clearly).

A good way to start: use it to save effort, and don't rush to let it decide things. Have it take the work from 0 to 70 points, drafting, organizing, finding ideas, which it does quickly and well; keep the last 30 points, checking facts, deciding what stays and what goes, and putting your name on it, for yourself.

## 4. "Does it have memory?" Get this clear on day one

Almost every newcomer gets the same wrong idea the first time: once the conversation gets going, you start to feel it knows you and remembers what you've talked about.

As Chapter 9 explained, the model itself has no memory across conversations; what looks like it "remembering" is the part the product puts back into the context. As of October 2026, many products, including Claude and ChatGPT, have project and memory features: projects let you keep materials and instructions together for reuse, and memory saves your preferences and key points from past chats, sometimes picking them out of your chats automatically, possibly with that switched on by default.

For your first conversations, that means three things:

- Don't assume it remembers. Give it the background it needs within this conversation.
- For preferences you want remembered long-term, use the product's memory or project features (Chapter 13 goes deeper).
- For things you don't want remembered, use a temporary chat (some products call it incognito), or turn memory off before chatting; now and then, check in the settings what it has saved and delete anything outdated or wrong. Names, defaults, and how to delete differ between products, so check each product's official description; for what shouldn't be sent to AI at all, see Chapter 37.

Start from "by default it has no memory," and you won't be puzzled when it suddenly doesn't seem to know you; but also know that a product with memory turned on may remember more than you think.

## 5. Common misconceptions, corrected

| Misconception | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| It knows everything and can be trusted as an authority | It's well-read but makes things up; it knows more than you, but what it says needs your check |
| Nothing it says is reliable, so I can't hand it work | Rewriting, drafting, organizing, and summarizing material are fast and good; only the hard facts need backstopping |
| It's like a search engine, so what it gives you are looked-up facts | It generates on the spot (Chapter 2) rather than looking things up; fluent doesn't mean correct (Chapters 4 and 9) |
| Writing tasks need no checking at all | Numbers, names, and sources slipped into the writing still need checking |
| Having chatted with it means it "knows" me | The model itself has no memory across conversations; a product's memory feature puts saved information back into the context, which isn't the model remembering |
| It should be able to guess what I want | What you don't spell out it can only guess; making your requirements clear is your job (Chapter 11) |

## Summary

- Treat it as a very well-read intern who now and then makes things up with a straight face, keeping both "well-read" and "makes things up" in mind. You're the supervisor: hand off the work freely, but your name and the consequences are yours.
- Rewriting, drafting, organizing, and summarizing material are safe to outsource; key numbers, sources, names and dates, and legal, medical, or financial conclusions are hard facts you must backstop, including any slipped into a piece of writing.
- Expect it to understand plain language, draft quickly, revise tirelessly, and work from your material; don't count on every sentence being true, on it remembering yesterday, knowing the latest news, or reading your mind.
- Use it to save effort and get the work to 70 points; keep the last 30 points, checking facts and making the calls, for yourself.
- By default it has no memory. Use memory or project features for long-term preferences (Chapter 13); for things you don't want remembered, use temporary chats or turn memory off, and look through and delete saved memories now and then.

The next chapter deals with the "it can't guess what you want" problem: how to say clearly what you want, which is the structure of a prompt.
