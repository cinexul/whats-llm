Many people have noticed this: learning an unfamiliar field with AI is remarkably efficient. A concept you couldn't untangle after staring at the textbook for ages often clicks once you ask it to "explain in plain language with an analogy." It never minds how naive your question is, and it's there at 2 a.m. As a reading buddy or private tutor, it's genuinely useful.

The same AI is also the tool most likely to lead you astray. The year, the quotation, the statistic it gives you may be made up on the spot, complete with names and convincing detail, and before you know it they're in your homework or your report. This chapter covers both sides: how to get the most out of it as a reading buddy and quiz-master, and why the facts it gives you have to be checked against the original source.

## 1. What it's best at: reading buddy, explaining, quizzing

| Use | How to use | Why it's good at it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading buddy / explaining | "I don't get this part, explain in plain language + an analogy" "Too shallow, go one level deeper" | It can phrase the same thing ten ways until you get it |
| Ask from another angle | "If you opposed this view, what would you say?" "Give three real-life examples" | It has seen a huge range of writing and is good at shifting perspective, giving examples, drawing analogies |
| Quiz you | "Write 5 questions on this content to test me" "Grade and explain after I answer" | It writes questions and gives feedback on the spot, which beats staring at the book |
| Sort out structure | "Sketch this chapter's logical skeleton as an outline" "How do I tell these concepts apart" | It's good at reorganizing scattered information into a clear framework |

What these uses have in common: the AI is helping you organize and re-express material you already have, not supplying new facts. Rephrasing, switching angles, writing questions and building frameworks are its strengths, and if an explanation goes wrong you'll usually notice right away. It's a buddy who explains well, not a teacher who holds the answer key.

## 2. The most dangerous: treating it as a "source of facts"

Anything that can be checked, such as specific years, names, quotations, statistics, sources, legal provisions and paper titles, has to be checked against the original source. Don't believe it just because it sounded sure.

The reason is hallucination, covered in Chapter 9: it doesn't check whether things are true as it writes, and when it isn't sure it may just keep going and produce the most reasonable-sounding answer. It can even make up a paper with a title, authors and a journal all in place.

So fluent and confident only means the words flowed smoothly; it says nothing about whether they're true. Picture a chatty acquaintance who retells things from memory and fills in whatever's fuzzy. When the retelling is right, the memory happened to be solid. When it's wrong, they don't know it, and they tell it with the same confidence.

```text
What you think it is:  question -> [verify -> give you the exact answer]
What it actually is:   question -> [continue from impression, fill gaps when unsure]
                                -> give you "something that looks like an answer"
```

When an explanation or a quiz question goes wrong, you can see it on the spot and little harm is done. When you put its years, quotes or figures in a report, you may not spot the error, and you'll assume "it said so, so it must be right."

## 3. So can you still use AI to look things up?

Yes, but in a different way: don't have it answer facts from memory; have it answer from reliable material you provide.

| The dangerous way (its "memory") | The safe way (based on "material") |
| --- | --- |
| "What year did Li Bai write this poem?" It fills in a guess | Paste in a reliable source: "answer based only on this material" |
| "What are the important studies on X?" It may invent papers that don't exist | Find real papers yourself, then: "help me understand and compare these" |
| "What does this law say?" It gives a plausible-sounding but wrong version | Find the actual text and paste it in; have it explain and give examples |

Two follow-up questions that always help:

- "What's the basis for that claim?" If it can't give a decent source, or the source turns out not to exist, be wary.
- "Answer only from the material I pasted; if it's not in there, say so." That closes off the room for guessing.

> **Key point:** Letting it answer facts from memory is betting that its memory happens to be right. Having it answer from real material you supply puts it back on what it's good at: understanding and organizing. RAG in Chapter 20 turns "supply reliable material first, then answer from it" into a system.

About web access: most mainstream chat assistants today (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and others) can search the web and attach source links to their answers. Links make checking easier, but they're only a starting point. Sometimes it cites a page that really exists and then "summarizes" something the page never says. Open the link and confirm the page actually supports the claim.

## 4. Hands-on: have it write 5 questions to test you, then grade and explain

This exercise uses both strengths, reading buddy and quiz-master, while avoiding the trap of treating it as a source of facts, because the questions and the answers all come from the same material you provide.

```text
Step one (write questions):
"Here's some material (pasted). Based on this material only, write 5 questions to test me,
 increasing in difficulty, covering the main concepts. Give only the questions for now,
 no answers."

Step two (you answer):
Answer all 5 yourself (this step forces you to actually understand, not skim past).

Step three (grade + explain):
"Here are my answers (pasted). Grade each one: what's right, what's wrong, and for the
 wrong ones, use the original words / basis from the material to explain clearly why
 I was wrong."

Step four (follow up on weak points):
For the one it flags as your weakest, follow up: "I still don't get this point,
 explain it again with a more concrete example."
```

It works for three reasons. Writing questions, giving instant feedback and re-explaining from a new angle are all its strengths. The questions and answers come from the same material, so it hardly needs to fill in outside facts. And answering first, then being graded, sticks far better than listening to an explanation; this is test-driven learning. Once you ask about a hard fact outside the material ("What year was this theory proposed?"), the rule from section 2 applies again: check the original source yourself.

## 5. Common misconceptions, corrected

| Misconception | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| It explains so clearly, so its facts must be right too | Clear explanation is a skill of expression; whether the facts are right is a separate question. Check hard facts yourself |
| It sounds very sure, so it must be true | Fluent and confident only means it flowed smoothly, not that it's correct |
| It gave me a paper or a quote, so I can copy it into my homework | Quotes, papers and statistics are the easiest things for it to invent; using them unchecked means vouching for its guesses |
| It gave me a link, so there's evidence | The link may be real while the content doesn't support its claim; open it and check |
| Learning with AI means having it explain the answer to me | It works better to have it quiz you, grade you and explain, so you do the understanding |

## Summary

- AI is excellent as a reading buddy, explainer and quiz-master, because all of these are organizing and expressing, which is what it's good at.
- Don't treat it as a source of facts: years, quotes, statistics and sources can come out looking completely real while being made up (Chapter 9). Check them against the original. More confident doesn't mean more correct.
- To look things up with it, have it answer from reliable material you paste in. Asking "what's your basis?" and "answer only from the material" is the same idea behind RAG (Chapter 20). Open the links it cites from web searches, too.
- The signature exercise: have it write 5 questions on a piece of material, answer them yourself, and let it grade and explain.

## Quiz

1. **[Basic · Concept]** Which of these is best handed to AI? <!-- quiz: q01 revision: 2 -->
   - A. Recommending authoritative papers to put straight into your references
   - B. Supplying the latest statistic to put straight into your report
   - C. Re-explaining a passage you don't get in plain words
   - D. Telling you the exact year and day a historical event happened
   > **Answer: C.** Rephrasing and comparisons are "organizing and expressing," exactly its strength, and if the explanation is off you'll usually notice right away. A, B, and D are all checkable hard facts, exactly where it's most likely to fill in gaps and most likely to burn you; check those against original sources yourself.

2. **[Basic · Concept]** Why can't the facts AI gives be trusted directly? <!-- quiz: q02 revision: 2 -->
   - A. Most training data comes from the web, so it absorbed every error online
   - B. When unsure, it doesn't stop; it spins out a plausible answer
   - C. To seem more helpful, it deliberately exaggerates or embellishes some facts
   - D. It knows facts only when online; offline, everything it says is a guess
   > **Answer: B.** Its job is to pick the most fitting next word (Chapter 4); it has no fact database to check against, and when unsure it fills in the answer that reads most plausibly (the hallucination in Chapter 9). A has some truth to it, but even with flawless training data it can still invent things. C anthropomorphizes it; there's no "deliberately." D is wrong too: offline it's often right, and online it can still misreport, so open the links and check.

3. **[Advanced · Misconception]** "It answered especially fluently and confidently, so the facts this time should be reliable." What's wrong? <!-- quiz: q03 revision: 2 -->
   - A. You didn't make it search the web first; answers after a search are reliable
   - B. You asked only once; if three tries give the same answer, it's reliable
   - C. Fluency and confidence only show it continued smoothly, not that it's true
   - D. The tone is the issue; ask it to answer again in a cautious tone instead
   > **Answer: C.** Fluency and correctness have no causal link; plenty of people are swayed by its assured tone into treating a made-up quote as real. A is wrong: even after searching it can misreport, so open the links and check. B: the same answer three times may just be the same error repeated. D only changes the wording, not whether the facts are right.

4. **[Advanced · Misconception]** The answer AI gave you has a link attached, looking quite official. You should? <!-- quiz: q04 revision: 2 -->
   - A. Open it and check that what it reported is really on that page
   - B. If the link opens and the site looks official, cite it with confidence
   - C. Just check the domain; a government or university site means it's reliable
   - D. Ask it to summarize the linked page again to confirm there's no mismatch
   > **Answer: A.** It sometimes gives a real web page yet "reports" content the page doesn't contain; a link existing doesn't mean the content is confirmed. (Web search and link features differ by product; check the official documentation.) B and C only confirm the link is real, not that the claim is. D has the same source vouch for itself again; you need to read the original yourself.

5. **[Basic · Scenario]** You're writing a course paper and want AI's help finding a few related studies to cite. What's the most reliable approach? <!-- quiz: q05 revision: 2 -->
   - A. Have it list papers, each with authors, year, and journal attached
   - B. Have it recommend some, then cite the ones that sound most authoritative
   - C. Have it recommend papers, then ask it "do these papers all really exist?"
   - D. Find real papers in proper databases, then have it help you compare
   > **Answer: D.** Asking it to list papers from memory easily produces ones that don't exist (a hotspot for hallucination). The right move is for you to supply the facts and let it do what it's good at: reading, comparing, organizing. A's authors and years just make an invented paper look more real. B picks by "sounds authoritative" without checking. C asks it to vouch for itself, and it may confidently say "yes" anyway. All of these can leave "ghost papers" that can't be found in your references.

6. **[Basic · Hands-on]** Find a piece of material you're learning (a textbook section or an article works), and do section 4 once: have it write 5 questions of increasing difficulty **based on this material only**, no answers yet; after you answer, have it grade each one **using the basis from the material** and explain clearly where you were wrong; finally, follow up on the weakest question and have it re-explain with another example. <!-- quiz: q06 revision: 2 -->
   > **What you should notice:** In this flow, both questions and answers are locked inside "the same material you gave," and it has almost no room to fill in external facts, so the grading is fairly trustworthy; writing questions, instant feedback, and explaining with another example are all its strengths. You'll get a real sense that "test-driven learning" sticks better than passive listening. The moment you ask about a hard fact outside the material, go back to the rule: **check the original source yourself.**
